Every driver in Israel knows the ritual: open the app, tap to start parking, hope you remember to stop the meter before you forget and it turns into a fine. We spent months digging into what actually happens behind that tap — and this week, that story reached far beyond academia.

**Mako**, one of Israel's biggest news sites, ran a feature bluntly titled *["The Bluff of Pango and CelloPark Exposed: How You're Paying More Money on Tickets"](https://www.mako.co.il/finances-money/Article-73676fd69c3af91026.htm)* (Hebrew), built entirely around our paper [Power and Ethical Concerns in the Integration of Smart City Technologies: A Case Study of Parking Payment Applications in Israel](https://doi.org/10.1080/10630732.2026.2655603), published in the *Journal of Urban Technology*.

The number that grabbed the headline is the one that surprised us too: the apps' entire pitch is that they make paying for parking easier, so tickets should go down. Instead, as parking-app revenue climbed across Israeli cities, fines climbed right along with it. More than a third of the users we surveyed had been ticketed despite using the app that very day — tripped up not by carelessness, but by the apps' own interface choices.

Mako's coverage zeroed in on the part of our findings that hits closest to home for ordinary drivers: the "dark patterns" baked into these apps, like premium "fine protection" subscriptions marketed by preying on the exact fear of getting ticketed that the apps themselves helped create. It's a strange business model — using the government's own punitive power as a sales funnel.

Seeing this research land in a national outlet, read by the very people who feel these design choices every time they park a car, is exactly why we did this work. Academic papers ask big questions about power, platforms, and public infrastructure — it's a rare and welcome thing when the answer reaches the people living the question.
